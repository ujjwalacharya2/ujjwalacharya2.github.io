Overview
What is this paper about?

David Donoho revisits 50 years of debate since John Tukey’s 1962 call to reform statistics. He argues that today’s “data science” movement is not new but the field’s true intellectual scope is still being missed.

Donoho proposes Greater Data Science (GDS): not just big data + ML, but the full science of learning from data, including a meta-science that studies data analysis itself.

“

I have come to feel that my central interest is in data analysis… procedures for analyzing data, techniques for interpreting the results… ways of planning the gathering of data…

- John Tukey, “The Future of Data Analysis,” 1962

Historical Context
50 years of calls to action
  • 1962● John Tukey -“The Future of Data Analysis”: data analysis as a new science, broader than mathematical statistics
  • 1993● John Chambers - “Greater or Lesser Statistics”: statistics must expand its boundaries or become marginal
  • 1997● Jeff Wu - proposes renaming statistics → “data science” in his Carver Lecture at University of Michigan
  • 2001● Bill Cleveland & Leo Breiman - action plans for expanding the field; two modeling cultures identified
  • 2015● Donoho - presented at Tukey Centennial; triggered by U of Michigan’s $100M Data Science Initiative
The Debate
Data science vs. statistics

Donoho dismantles three popular “memes” used to separate data science from statistics , and shows each fails under scrutiny:

  • “Big Data” meme - statistics always dealt with large-scale data; the word “statistics” itself comes from census data
  • “Skills” meme - Hadoop/Spark are organizational coping tools, not deep intellectual advances
  • “Jobs” meme - industry hiring demand does not define or create a new science
“

Data Scientist (n.): Person who is better at statistics than any software engineer and better at software engineering than any statistician.

- popular tech industry quip, cited by Donoho

Simple Methods Often Win
Glamour ≠ performance

A key empirical finding: theoretically sophisticated methods frequently fail to outperform simple baselines in real cross-study tests.

  • Hand (2006): Linear discriminant analysis (Fisher, 1936) achieved 90%+ of achievable improvement on benchmark datasets - despite decades of fancier methods.
  • Donoho & Jin (2008): A dead-simple ±1 weight classifier matched or beat random forests and boosting across standard ML benchmarks.
  • Zhao et al. (2014): The Más-o-Menos classifier matched lasso and ridge across clinical cancer genomics studies.

The lesson: cross-study variation, not algorithmic complexity, drives real-world performance differences.

Breiman’s Two Cultures
Two ways to learn from data

Leo Breiman (2001) identified a fundamental split in how analysts approach data , Donoho argues both belong inside GDS:

Generative Modeling

  • Stochastic models that could have generated the data
  • Focus on inference & uncertainty
  • Traditional academic statistics (~98% of courses in 2001)

Predictive Modeling

  • Methods that predict well on a specific dataset
  • Accuracy over interpretability
  • Machine learning & industrial AI

Donoho: the Common Task Framework - shared data + objective leaderboard - is the “secret sauce” behind ML’s industrial success, not algorithmic breakthroughs alone.

Key Mechanism
The Common Task Framework (CTF)

Donoho identifies CTF as the most underappreciated concept in data science , the true engine behind ML progress in Netflix Prize, ImageNet, Kaggle, and DARPA challenges:

  • AA publicly available training dataset with features and class labels, shared openly with all competitors
  • BA set of enrolled competitors who independently develop prediction rules from the shared data
  • CAn objective scoring referee that tests submissions on a sequestered holdout set and reports accuracy automatically

Pattern of results: error rates decline by a fixed percentage each year; progress comes from many small improvements; shared data gets reused in unexpected ways.

Google Translate, smartphone Face ID, and voice assistants all exist because of decades of CTF competition - not isolated algorithmic breakthroughs alone.

The Six Divisions
Greater Data Science (GDS)

Donoho merges Chambers & Cleveland’s frameworks into six divisions of a fully realized data science. Most programs today teach only GDS5:

GDS 1Data Gathering, Prep & Exploration - cleaning, wrangling, EDA (~80% of real effort)
GDS 2Data Representation & Transformation - SQL, NoSQL, Fourier transforms, tidy data
GDS 3Computing with Data - R, Python, pipelines, clusters, reproducible workflows
GDS 4Data Visualization & Presentation - EDA plots, dashboards, communicating findings
GDS 5Data Modeling - generative (statistics) + predictive (ML) cultures
GDS 6Science about Data Science - meta-analysis, reproducibility, evidence-based methodology

~80% of real data science effort is spent in GDS1 - yet this is the division most neglected in curricula. Students who only ever analyze pre-cooked datasets miss the hardest and most important skills.

Key Finding
Science itself becomes data

Donoho’s most visionary claim: GDS6, Science about Data Science , will become the most important frontier as all of science becomes a body of data we can mine and study.

>1M scientific papers published annually in clinical research alone , each a data point about methodology

~50% of published findings may be incorrect or irreproducible (Ioannidis et al.)

Cross-study analysis (Parmigiani et al.): Applying 14 models across 10 independent ovarian cancer datasets (1,251 patients) revealed the best model in the literature was being systematically underused.

Cross-workflow analysis (Madigan/OMOP): Across 200M+ patient-years, the choice of analysis workflow alone reverses published conclusions about the same drug.

“

The really important intellectual event of the next 50 years is not about scaling up , but the emergence of scientific studies of data analysis science-wide.

- Donoho (2017), paraphrased

Looking Ahead
Data Science in 2065
  • 1Open science takes over - code & data sharing become universal; reproducibility enforced by journals and funding agencies
  • 2Science becomes data - every published result becomes programmatically citable and retrievable; meta-analysis spans all of science
  • 3Empirical validation replaces proof - method performance validated across entire corpora of real datasets, not under narrow theoretical assumptions

In 2065, asking “which method is best?” will be answered empirically across thousands of real studies , not by mathematical derivation alone.

Significance & Critique
Why this paper matters
Strengths: Synthesizes 50 years of intellectual history into a coherent non-commercial vision; CTF insight is original and well-evidenced; GDS framework is directly actionable for curriculum design.
Limitations: Underplays data engineering (acknowledged in epilogue); written primarily for academic statisticians; GDS vision remains largely aspirational , most programs still teach primarily GDS5.
Relevance today: The reproducibility crisis, open science movement, and dominance of R/Python all directly vindicate Donoho’s 2017 predictions.
Course Connection
Why this matters for us

This paper directly motivates why we use R, Quarto, and reproducible workflows in this course. GDS3 (computing with data) and GDS1 (data preparation and exploration) are the foundations of modern data science practice , exactly what an advanced computing course in R is designed to build.

Every time we write a reproducible R Markdown document, share code, or wrangle messy data, we are practicing the Greater Data Science that Donoho envisioned.
